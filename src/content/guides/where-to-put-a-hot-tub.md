Where the hot tub goes decides a lot: how much the install costs, how often you use it, how easy it is to service and whether your deck is still level in five years. It's also hard to change later. A few hours of planning here saves a lot of regret.

## Where is the best place to put a hot tub?

**The best spot is a level, load-rated surface close to the house, with GFCI-protected power, room for service access, good drainage and some privacy from neighbors and wind.**

In practice, that usually means a concrete patio or dedicated pad just off the back door. Owner feedback across r/hottubs is consistent on one point: tubs placed far from the house get used less, especially in winter.

## How much does a filled hot tub weigh?

**Filled weight = gallons × 8.34 lb + the tub's dry weight + the occupants.** A typical hard-shell exceeds two tons when filled and occupied; even an inflatable can exceed a ton.

Water weighs about 8.34 pounds per gallon. Check the manufacturer's spec sheet for gallons and dry weight, and assume a realistic weight per occupant.

### Worked examples

| | 250-gal inflatable | 400-gal hard-shell |
|---|---|---|
| Water (gallons × 8.34) | 2,085 lb | 3,336 lb |
| Dry weight (assumption; see spec sheet) | ~70 lb | ~800 lb |
| Occupants (4 × 175 lb, assumption) | 700 lb | 700 lb |
| **Total filled weight** | **~2,855 lb** | **~4,836 lb** |
| Footprint (assumption) | ~6.5 ft round (~33 sq ft) | 7 × 7 ft (49 sq ft) |
| **Load per square foot** | **~87 lb/sq ft** | **~99 lb/sq ft** |

Those per-square-foot numbers matter. Typical residential deck design loads are often in the range of about 40 lb/sq ft live load plus the deck's own weight, far below what a hot tub concentrates in one spot. That's why a deck install almost always needs structural review.

## Can you put a hot tub on a deck?

**Only if the deck is designed or reinforced for the tub's filled weight, and a structural engineer or qualified contractor has confirmed it.** Don't assume an existing deck can take it.

What a deck install typically involves:

- **Engineering review** of joists, beams, posts and footings under the tub's footprint.
- **Added footings and posts** directly beneath the tub.
- **Permits**, since many jurisdictions require them for structural changes.
- **Moisture planning**, because splash and cover drips over years can rot wood around the tub.

A common alternative is to build the deck around a tub sitting on its own concrete pad at grade, which gets the look without putting the load on the deck.

## What is the best base for a hot tub?

**A level, reinforced concrete pad is the most common and widely recommended base, especially for hard-shells.** Compacted gravel and engineered spa pads work for some tubs; check the manual.

| Base | Best for | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reinforced concrete pad | Hard-shells, permanent installs | Strong, level, long-lasting | Highest cost; permanent |
| Existing concrete patio | Hard-shells and inflatables | No new construction | Must be level, crack-free and thick enough |
| Compacted gravel with border | Some hard-shells, inflatables | Good drainage, lower cost | Needs careful leveling; not approved by all makers |
| Prefab spa pad (interlocking panels) | Lighter tubs, some hard-shells | Quick DIY install | Load and warranty limits vary |
| Reinforced deck | Hard-shells, inflatables | Integrated look | Requires structural review |

For inflatables, add the manufacturer's ground mat or a foam underlay. It protects the floor from abrasion and reduces heat loss into cold ground. Many hard-shell warranties specify an approved base, so read the requirements before you pour or build.

## What electrical requirements does a hot tub need?

**Hot tubs require GFCI protection under the National Electrical Code, and most full-size hard-shells need a dedicated 240V circuit installed by a licensed electrician.** Inflatables and plug-and-play models use a GFCI-protected 120V outlet.

NEC Article 680 governs pools, spas and hot tubs. Among other things, it requires ground-fault protection and typically calls for a disconnect within sight of the tub but at least 5 feet away from it. Local codes add their own requirements, and inspections are common.

Practical points:

- **Hire a licensed electrician** for any new circuit, disconnect or hardwired tub. This isn't a DIY job.
- **Avoid extension cords** for plug-in tubs. Manufacturer manuals typically prohibit them.
- **Keep clear of overhead power lines.** Codes set minimum clearances; don't place a tub under lines.
- **Plan the wire run.** A spot closer to your main panel can reduce the cost of a 240V circuit.
- **Get a permit** if required. Your electrician will know.

If you're choosing between tub types partly based on wiring, see [inflatable vs hard-shell hot tubs](/blog/inflatable-vs-hard-shell-hot-tub).

## How much space do you need around a hot tub?

**Leave room to walk around it, open the service panels, remove the cover and reach the filter.** Service access is easy to overlook and expensive to fix.

- **Service panels:** Most hard-shells need access to at least one side, often more, for repairs. Check the manual.
- **Cover clearance:** A cover lifter needs space behind the tub, often 18 inches or more depending on the model.
- **Steps and handrails:** Plan for a stable step and a hand-hold for getting in and out.
- **Walkway:** Enough room to walk around safely with wet feet.

## What else should you consider when choosing a spot?

**Think about drainage, privacy, wind, sun, the view and the path from the house.** These shape how often you use the tub.

- **Drainage.** Water will splash and you'll need to drain the tub several times a year. Make sure the area slopes away from the house and that you have somewhere suitable to drain treated water. See [how often to change hot tub water](/blog/how-often-to-change-hot-tub-water).
- **Wind.** A sheltered spot loses less heat. See [how much it costs to run a hot tub](/blog/how-much-does-it-cost-to-run-a-hot-tub).
- **Privacy.** Check sightlines from neighbors' windows and second floors, not just the fence line.
- **Trees.** Overhanging branches drop leaves and sap into the water and onto the cover.
- **Sun.** Direct sun helps in winter but speeds UV damage on covers and inflatable walls.
- **Path from the door.** Short, well-lit and non-slip. This is the single biggest factor in winter use.
- **Water access.** A nearby hose bib makes refilling easier.
- **Rules.** Check HOA rules, setbacks and any local barrier or fencing requirements.

## Can you put a hot tub indoors?

**Yes, but indoor tubs need a floor rated for the load, a drain, moisture-resistant surfaces and serious ventilation.** Humidity is the main challenge.

An indoor hot tub releases a lot of moisture. Without proper ventilation and dehumidification, it can damage drywall, wood and finishes and encourage mold. Floors must handle the filled weight, just like a deck. Indoor installs typically need a contractor, electrician and sometimes an HVAC specialist. Make sure the tub is rated for indoor use.

## Where should an inflatable hot tub go?

**On a flat, smooth, level surface that can bear the filled weight, near a GFCI-protected outdoor outlet, and never directly on grass or bare soil.**

Inflatables are forgiving about location, but not about ground conditions. Sharp stones, uneven pavers and slopes stress the floor and walls. A level concrete patio with a ground mat is ideal.
