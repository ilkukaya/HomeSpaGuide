This is the first fork in the road for almost every hot tub buyer. The two types solve the same problem in very different ways, and the right answer depends less on which is "better" and more on your budget, climate, space and how sure you are that you'll use the thing.

## Should you buy an inflatable or hard-shell hot tub?

**Buy an inflatable if you want low cost, easy setup and flexibility; buy a hard-shell if you want year-round performance, stronger jets and a long lifespan.** Many buyers start with an inflatable and move up once they know the habit sticks.

Our [first home spa guide](/guides/choose-first-home-spa) walks through the full buying framework. This article focuses on the head-to-head.

## How do inflatable and hard-shell hot tubs compare?

**Hard-shells win on performance, insulation and lifespan; inflatables win on price, portability and setup.** Here's the side-by-side:

| Factor | Inflatable | Hard-shell |
|---|---|---|
| Typical price bucket | ~$250–$1,000 | ~$2,500–$5,000 for entry models; $5,000+ common |
| Power | 120V standard outlet (GFCI-protected) | Mostly 240V dedicated circuit; some 120V plug-and-play |
| Installation | DIY in an afternoon | Delivery, site prep, often an electrician |
| Heat-up rate (spec sheets) | ~1.5–3°F per hour | ~3–6°F per hour on 240V |
| Insulation | Thin walls, soft or thin cover | Foam insulation, tapered hard cover |
| Jets | Mostly air bubble rings; some models add a few water jets | Many directed water jets, often adjustable |
| Seating | Open floor, no molded seats | Molded seats, loungers, varied depths |
| Winter use | Limited; most must be drained and stored | Designed for year-round use |
| Lifespan (owner reports) | ~3–6 seasons | ~10–20 years with maintenance |
| Portability | Can be drained, deflated and moved | Effectively permanent |
| Filled weight | Lower, but still heavy | Much heavier; needs a rated surface |

## How much does each type cost to buy and run?

**Inflatables are far cheaper to buy; hard-shells are often cheaper to run each month in cold climates.** Over several years, the gap narrows but rarely closes.

The upfront difference is large. Most inflatables fall in the $250–$1,000 price range, while hard-shells commonly start around $2,500 and quickly move into the $5,000+ bracket. Add delivery, a pad or deck reinforcement and electrical work for a hard-shell.

Running costs flip the picture. Hard-shells have foam insulation and thick covers, so their heaters run in short bursts. Inflatables lose heat through thin walls and lids, and their 120V heaters have to run longer to keep up. Our [running cost breakdown](/blog/how-much-does-it-cost-to-run-a-hot-tub) walks through the kWh math with examples for both.

## Which heats faster and stays warmer?

**Hard-shells heat faster and hold heat far better, especially below about 50°F.** A 240V heater can deliver several times the power of a 120V unit.

Manufacturer spec sheets for 120V inflatables typically list 1.5 to 3°F of heating per hour, while 240V hard-shells commonly list 3 to 6°F. Many inflatables also switch off the heater while the bubble system runs, so temperature can drop during a long soak in cold weather. For formulas and tips, see [how long a hot tub takes to heat up](/blog/how-long-does-a-hot-tub-take-to-heat-up).

## Which has better jets and comfort?

**Hard-shells offer targeted water jets and molded seats; most inflatables offer an all-over bubble massage on a flat floor.**

Inflatables typically use an air-bubble ring around the floor, which feels fizzy and relaxing rather than deep-tissue. A few premium inflatables add a handful of water jets. Hard-shells use pumps to drive water through dozens of jets placed at the back, shoulders and feet, and molded seats put your body in the right position for them. If massage is your priority, owner feedback strongly favors hard-shells.

## Which is easier to install?

**Inflatables are far easier: set on a flat surface, inflate, fill and plug in.** Hard-shells need planning for delivery access, a load-rated surface and usually a dedicated electrical circuit.

For an inflatable, you need a level, smooth surface that can handle the filled weight and a GFCI-protected outdoor outlet. For a hard-shell, you need a path wide enough for delivery, a concrete pad, reinforced deck or engineered base, and in most cases a 240V circuit installed by a licensed electrician. Our guide to [where to put a hot tub](/guides/where-to-put-a-hot-tub) covers weight math, surfaces and electrical clearances.

## Which lasts longer?

**Hard-shells last many times longer.** Owner reports put inflatables at roughly three to six seasons and well-maintained hard-shells at 10 to 20 years.

Inflatables usually fail at the pump and heater unit, at seams, or from UV damage and winter storage creases. Hard-shells need occasional repairs (pumps, heaters, control boards, covers), but the shell itself can last decades. Careful winter storage extends inflatable life a lot; see [how to winterize an inflatable hot tub](/blog/inflatable-hot-tub-winter-prep).

## Is water care different between them?

**The chemistry is the same, but inflatables need more frequent filter changes and water changes.** They hold less water and use smaller filters, so contaminants build up faster.

Both types need the same targets: pH 7.2–7.8, total alkalinity 80–120 ppm and sanitizer in range. Our [chemicals beginner's guide](/guides/hot-tub-chemicals-beginners-guide) and [how to keep your hot tub water clean](/guides/keep-hot-tub-water-clean) apply to both. For water-change timing, see [how often to change hot tub water](/blog/how-often-to-change-hot-tub-water).

## What about 120V plug-and-play hard-shells?

**Plug-and-play hard-shells are a middle ground: a rigid, insulated shell with molded seats that runs on a standard 120V GFCI-protected outlet.** They avoid the cost of new 240V wiring but inherit some 120V limitations.

Their heaters are limited by the circuit, so heat-up is slower than a 240V tub, and many switch the heater off while the jet pump runs on high. In cold weather, that can mean temperature drifts down during a long soak. Most can be converted to 240V later by an electrician, which some manufacturers allow and some don't; check the manual before you buy if that flexibility matters. For buyers who want real seats and better insulation without an electrical project on day one, they're worth a look in the [hot tubs category](/category/hot-tubs).

## Who should buy which?

**Match the tub to your climate, budget and certainty.** Here's a quick guide:

| If you… | Lean toward |
|---|---|
| Aren't sure you'll use a hot tub regularly | Inflatable |
| Rent, or may move within a few years | Inflatable |
| Have a budget under the $1,000 range | Inflatable |
| Want year-round soaking in a cold climate | Hard-shell |
| Want strong, targeted massage jets | Hard-shell |
| Plan to stay in your home for a decade | Hard-shell |
| Want a middle ground without 240V wiring | 120V plug-and-play hard-shell |
