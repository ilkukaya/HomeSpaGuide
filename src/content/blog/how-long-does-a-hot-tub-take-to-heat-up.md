You've filled the tub, the control panel says 58°F and you'd like to soak tonight. Whether that's realistic depends on your heater, how much water you're warming and how much heat escapes along the way. Here's how to estimate it before you start, and what speeds it up.

## How long does a hot tub take to heat up?

**Most 240V hard-shell hot tubs heat about 3 to 6°F per hour, and most 120V inflatables about 1.5 to 3°F per hour.** From cold tap water, that's roughly 8 to 16 hours for a hard-shell and 12 to 36 hours for an inflatable.

Those ranges come from manufacturer spec sheets across the category, and owner reviews broadly support them in mild weather. Cold air, wind and an open cover all slow things down.

| Tub type | Typical heater | Typical heat-up rate | 50°F to 100°F (50°F rise) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 120V inflatable | ~1.3–2.2 kW | ~1.5–3°F/hour | ~17–33 hours |
| 120V plug-and-play hard-shell | ~1–1.5 kW (heater often limited when jets run) | ~1–3°F/hour | ~17–50 hours |
| 240V hard-shell | ~4–6 kW | ~3–6°F/hour | ~8–17 hours |

## How can you calculate your hot tub's heat-up time?

**Energy needed (kWh) = gallons × 8.34 × degrees of temperature rise ÷ 3,412. Divide that by the heater's kW rating to get hours, before heat loss.**

The numbers behind it: one gallon of water weighs about 8.34 pounds, raising one pound of water 1°F takes one BTU, and one kWh equals about 3,412 BTU.

### Worked example: 250-gallon inflatable

- Volume: 250 gallons
- Start: 55°F, target: 102°F, so a 47°F rise
- Heater: 1.5 kW (check your spec sheet)

250 × 8.34 × 47 ÷ 3,412 = **about 28.7 kWh**
28.7 kWh ÷ 1.5 kW = **about 19 hours**, before losses

Real-world time will be longer, because the tub loses heat while it warms. On a mild day with the cover on, a few extra hours is typical. On a cold, windy day, it can take much longer, and some owner reviews describe inflatables that struggle to gain temperature at all in freezing wind.

### Worked example: 400-gallon hard-shell

- Volume: 400 gallons
- Start: 55°F, target: 102°F, so a 47°F rise
- Heater: 4 kW

400 × 8.34 × 47 ÷ 3,412 = **about 46 kWh**
46 kWh ÷ 4 kW = **about 11.5 hours**, before losses

Hard-shell tubs lose less heat thanks to foam insulation and thicker covers, so their real-world times are usually closer to the calculated figure. For what this energy costs, see [how much it costs to run a hot tub](/blog/how-much-does-it-cost-to-run-a-hot-tub).

## Why do inflatable hot tubs heat more slowly?

**They're limited by a 120V circuit and lose heat faster through thinner walls and covers.** The heater can only draw so much power from a standard outlet.

A typical 15-amp or 20-amp 120V circuit caps how much wattage the heater and pump can pull together. That's why many inflatables and plug-and-play hard-shells automatically switch the heater off while jets or air blowers run: the circuit can't power everything at once. It's also why heating an inflatable in cold weather can feel like a stalemate. If slow heating is a deal-breaker, compare the two tub types in our [inflatable vs hard-shell guide](/blog/inflatable-vs-hard-shell-hot-tub).

## What slows down hot tub heating?

**An uncovered tub, cold and windy weather, a dirty filter and running jets while heating are the most common culprits.**

- **Cover off or poorly fitted.** Most heat escapes from the water surface. Keep the cover on and latched.
- **Cold air and wind.** The bigger the gap between water and air, the faster heat escapes.
- **Cold ground.** Inflatables sitting directly on concrete or cold ground lose heat downward.
- **Dirty filter.** Reduced flow can make the heater cycle off or trip a flow sensor. See [how to clean a hot tub filter](/guides/how-to-clean-hot-tub-filter).
- **Jets or blower running.** On many 120V tubs, this pauses the heater.
- **Low water level.** Some heaters won't run if water is below the minimum line.

## How can you heat a hot tub faster?

**Keep the cover on, add a floating thermal blanket, block the wind, keep the filter clean and avoid running jets until you reach temperature.**

1. **Cover it immediately after filling.** Start heating with the lid on and closed.
2. **Add a thermal blanket.** A floating bubble-style blanket under the cover reduces evaporation and heat loss. Find them in [covers and accessories](/category/covers-accessories).
3. **Insulate underneath.** For inflatables, use the manufacturer's ground mat or a foam underlay.
4. **Shelter from wind.** Temporary screens help when heating in cool weather.
5. **Fill during the warmest part of the day.** Warmer air slows heat loss, and in summer, water sitting in a sunny hose line starts a little warmer.
6. **Check your manual about warm fill water.** Some manufacturers set a maximum fill temperature to protect components, so don't assume hot water from the house is fine.
7. **Leave the jets off** until the target temperature is reached.

## Should you keep a hot tub hot all the time?

**For regular users, keeping the tub at or near soaking temperature is usually more practical than reheating from cold each time.** Reheating hundreds of gallons takes many hours and a big chunk of energy.

Many owners hold the water a few degrees below their soaking temperature between uses, then raise it an hour or two before getting in. Manufacturer manuals often include an economy or sleep mode for exactly this purpose. In freezing climates, keeping the water warm also protects the plumbing; for inflatables that aren't freeze-rated, see [our winterizing guide](/blog/inflatable-hot-tub-winter-prep).

## What is the maximum temperature for a hot tub?

**The CPSC recommends that hot tub water never exceed 104°F.** Most tubs cap their thermostats at 104°F for this reason.

Many adults find 100 to 102°F comfortable for longer soaks. For guidance on time limits and who should take extra care, see [hot tub safety: temperature and time limits](/blog/hot-tub-safety-temperature-time-limits).
