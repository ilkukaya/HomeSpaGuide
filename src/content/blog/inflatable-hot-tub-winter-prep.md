Most inflatable spas are not freeze-rated. Leave one outside through a Minnesota winter and you'll spend the spring shopping for a new tub — the pump housing cracks first, then the inflatable ring sets in a permanent crease where the water level was when it froze. The good news: the four-step shutdown is the same across nearly every brand, and owners typically describe it as a job of an hour or two the first time.

## When to start

Watch the overnight low. Once it's reliably below 50°F for a week — typically late October in the upper Midwest, early December in the Pacific Northwest, sometime never in the southern half of the US — it's time. Don't wait for the first frost. Cold pump motors plus residual water in the heat lines is what cracks the housing, and that happens at 30°F, not 0°F.

## Step 1 — Drain it correctly

Hook a garden hose to the drain port. **Drain into a yard, not a storm drain** — bromine and chlorine residuals are problematic for treated water systems. While draining, run the pump for the last five minutes at no-heat mode to clear water out of the heating element. Stop when air starts to spit at the drain valve.

## Step 2 — Dry everything

Wipe the inside with a microfiber towel. Pop the filter cartridge out and rinse it; let it air-dry on a wire rack for 24 hours before storing. The pump unit needs to drain by gravity — disconnect the hoses at both ports, lift the heater outlet end about 6 inches higher than the inlet, and let it sit overnight on a folded towel.

## Step 3 — Fold and store

Wait until the tub is fully dry inside. Fold according to the manufacturer's pattern (usually a "trifold then roll" sequence) — don't improvise. The factory pattern keeps the air-beam ring from creasing in the same spot two years running. Store somewhere that stays above 32°F: an unheated garage in most US climates is fine; a shed in Wisconsin is not.

## Step 4 — Cover the pump

Even with the spa packed up, the pump unit is worth its own dust bag. A plain garbage bag rubber-banded at both ends is enough — the goal is to keep airborne dust off the heater-plug contacts so it powers up cleanly when you unpack it.

## The freeze-rated exception

If your spa is one of the few inflatables with a built-in freeze-shield mode — such as the EnergySense versions of the Bestway SaluSpa Hawaii and Milan or the Coleman SaluSpa Tahiti — you can leave it running through the winter without draining. Freeze-shield cycles the pump intermittently below ~40°F to keep water moving through the heat lines, which is enough to prevent the cracking failure mode. Expect a noticeably higher electricity bill through the cold months than draining and storing would cost (see [how much it costs to run a hot tub](/blog/how-much-does-it-cost-to-run-a-hot-tub) to estimate it for your climate), but you keep your hot tub in use.

## What goes wrong if you skip a step

Customer reports on r/hottubs and the Lay-Z-Spa owner forums consistently describe the same failure modes when one of these steps gets skipped: cracked heater housings the following spring (residual water in the heat lines, almost always traceable to a missed gravity-drain), and permanent creases in the air-beam ring at the old waterline (improvised folding, or folding while still damp). The spas that get stored to spec almost never appear in those threads.

It's an hour or two once a year. Don't skip it.
