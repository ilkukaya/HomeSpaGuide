The electricity bill is the running cost most new owners underestimate, and it is the one they ask about most. The good news is that you don't have to guess. With two numbers from your spa's spec sheet and one from your utility bill, you can estimate the monthly cost within a reasonable range before you buy.

## How much does it cost to run a hot tub per month?

**Most home hot tubs cost roughly $20–$60 a month in electricity.** A well-insulated hard-shell spa in a mild climate usually sits at the low end. A 120V inflatable running through a northern winter can exceed the top of that range.

The spread is wide because the bill is driven by heat loss, and heat loss depends on things that vary from house to house: how cold it gets outside, how good the cover is, how warm you keep the water and how often the cover comes off. That's why a single "average cost" number is less useful than learning the calculation.

## How do you calculate hot tub running costs?

**Monthly cost = kilowatt-hours (kWh) used per month × your electricity rate per kWh.** To get kWh, multiply each component's wattage by the hours it runs, then divide by 1,000.

Here's the breakdown of where the watts go:

| Component | Typical draw | How long it runs |
|---|---|---|
| Heater (120V inflatable or plug-and-play) | ~1,300–2,200 W | Varies with heat loss; often several hours a day in cool weather |
| Heater (240V hard-shell) | ~4,000–6,000 W | Runs in shorter bursts because it heats faster |
| Circulation / filtration pump | ~200–800 W | Scheduled filter cycles, often several hours a day |
| Jet pumps and air blower | ~800–2,000 W+ | Only while you're soaking |
| LED lighting | Under 20 W | Negligible |

Wattages above are typical of manufacturer spec sheets across the category; your spa's label or manual lists the exact numbers.

Your electricity rate is printed on your utility bill as a price per kWh. Use the all-in figure (energy plus delivery charges) if your bill separates them, because that's what you actually pay per extra kWh.

## What does a worked example look like?

**Assumption for both examples: an electricity rate of $0.17 per kWh.** That's a round number close to recent US residential averages; replace it with the rate on your own bill.

### Example 1: 120V inflatable, cool spring weather

- Heater: 1,500 W, running an average of 6 hours a day to hold temperature (assumption)
- Filtration pump: 300 W, running 8 hours a day (assumption)

Heater: 1,500 W × 6 h × 30 days ÷ 1,000 = **270 kWh**
Pump: 300 W × 8 h × 30 days ÷ 1,000 = **72 kWh**
Total: 342 kWh × $0.17 = **about $58 a month**

Drop the heater to 3 hours a day in summer and the bill falls to roughly $35. Push it to 12 hours a day in a freezing January and it climbs past $100. That swing is why owner reviews of inflatables report such different numbers depending on where they live.

### Example 2: Insulated 240V hard-shell, same weather

- Heater: 4,000 W, running an average of 1.5 hours a day (assumption; better insulation means shorter heating bursts)
- Circulation pump: 250 W, running 12 hours a day (assumption)

Heater: 4,000 W × 1.5 h × 30 ÷ 1,000 = **180 kWh**
Pump: 250 W × 12 h × 30 ÷ 1,000 = **90 kWh**
Total: 270 kWh × $0.17 = **about $46 a month**

The hard-shell's heater draws nearly three times the wattage, yet it uses less energy overall because it loses less heat. Wattage tells you how fast a heater works, not how much it costs to run.

## Is an inflatable or hard-shell hot tub cheaper to run?

**Per month, a well-insulated hard-shell is usually cheaper to run; per year including purchase, an inflatable is usually cheaper to own.** The gap in running costs grows the colder your climate is.

| Factor | Inflatable | Hard-shell |
|---|---|---|
| Wall insulation | Air or foam-lined vinyl, relatively thin | Full or perimeter foam insulation, often several inches |
| Cover | Soft or thin foam lid | Tapered foam cover, often 4 inches or more at the center |
| Heater power | ~1,300–2,200 W on 120V | ~4,000–6,000 W on 240V (plug-and-play models are lower) |
| Cold-weather bill | Rises sharply below ~50°F | Rises gradually |
| Upfront cost bucket | Typically $250–$1,000 | Typically $2,500 and up |

## What makes a hot tub more expensive to run?

**Heat loss is the main driver: cold air, wind, a poor cover and long uncovered sessions all make the heater work harder.** Everything else is secondary.

The biggest factors, roughly in order:

1. **Outdoor temperature.** The difference between 104°F water and 30°F air is more than double the difference in 70°F weather, so heat escapes far faster.
2. **Cover condition.** A waterlogged, warped or poorly sealed cover leaks heat constantly. Owner reports across r/hottubs regularly describe bills dropping noticeably after replacing a failing cover.
3. **Set temperature.** Holding 104°F costs more than holding 100°F. The difference compounds every hour of every day.
4. **Wind exposure.** Wind strips heat from the cover surface and any gaps around it.
5. **Usage.** Every uncovered minute lets heat and water vapor escape, and jets and blowers draw extra power while they run.
6. **Water volume.** A larger tub holds more water to keep warm and has more surface area to lose heat through.

## How can you lower your hot tub's electricity bill?

**Keep a well-fitting cover on whenever the tub isn't in use, lower the set point when you're not soaking, and block the wind.** Those three habits do most of the work.

- **Upgrade or maintain the cover.** Check that it sits flush and that the foam cores haven't absorbed water (a heavy cover is a waterlogged cover). Browse options in [covers and accessories](/category/covers-accessories).
- **Add a floating thermal blanket.** It sits on the water under the main cover and reduces evaporation, which is a meaningful source of heat loss.
- **Use a windbreak.** A fence panel, hedge or privacy screen on the windward side helps more than most owners expect.
- **Schedule filtration for off-peak hours.** If your utility uses time-of-use pricing, running filter cycles overnight can shave the bill without changing anything else.
- **Keep the filter clean.** A clogged filter makes the pump work harder and can trip heater flow sensors. See [how to clean a hot tub filter](/guides/how-to-clean-hot-tub-filter).
- **Insulate underneath.** For inflatables, the manufacturer's ground mat or a foam underlay reduces heat lost into cold concrete or ground.
- **Pick the right tub for your climate.** In a cold region, a heavily insulated hard-shell from the [hot tubs category](/category/hot-tubs) may pay back its higher price in lower bills over several years.

## Is it cheaper to keep a hot tub on all the time?

**For people who soak several times a week, yes, in most cases.** Reheating 300 gallons from cold takes a large chunk of energy in one go, and the tub will lose that heat again anyway if you let it cool.

The math behind this: heating water takes about 8.34 BTU per gallon per degree Fahrenheit, and one kWh equals about 3,412 BTU. Raising 300 gallons by 40°F needs roughly 100,000 BTU, or about 29 kWh before any losses. For more on heat-up times, see [how long a hot tub takes to heat up](/blog/how-long-does-a-hot-tub-take-to-heat-up).

If you'll be gone for a week or more, most manufacturer manuals suggest an economy or vacation mode that holds the water several degrees lower rather than shutting down entirely, especially in freezing weather where a cold tub can freeze its plumbing. For inflatables heading into winter, [our winterizing guide](/blog/inflatable-hot-tub-winter-prep) explains when draining makes more sense.

## What other running costs should you budget for?

**Beyond electricity, plan for water care chemicals, filter cartridges, periodic water changes and eventually a replacement cover.** These are smaller than the power bill but add up.

- **Chemicals:** sanitizer, shock, pH and alkalinity adjusters and test strips. Our [chemicals beginner's guide](/guides/hot-tub-chemicals-beginners-guide) lists the starter kit, and the [chemicals and water care](/category/chemicals-water-care) category has the options.
- **Filters:** cartridges wear out and need replacing on a schedule. See [filters and filtration](/category/filters-filtration).
- **Water:** refilling a few hundred gallons several times a year is a small water bill line item. How often depends on use; see [how often to change hot tub water](/blog/how-often-to-change-hot-tub-water).

Run the numbers with your own rate and climate, and you'll know before you buy whether the tub you're considering fits the budget month after month.
